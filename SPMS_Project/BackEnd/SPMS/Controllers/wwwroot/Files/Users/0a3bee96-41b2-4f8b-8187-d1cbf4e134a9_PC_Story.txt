Vikram and Vetaal: The Three Suitors

The Beginning

Long ago, in the ancient city of Ujjain, there ruled a king whose name has survived through the ages.

His name was King Vikramaditya.

He was renowned not only for his courage, but also for his wisdom and his unfailing sense of justice.

Yet there was one strange task that even such a king found difficult.

Every night, King Vikramaditya would enter a dark and lonely forest to capture a mysterious spirit known as Vetaal.

The spirit lived within a corpse that hung from a great tree in a cremation ground.

The king had been commanded to bring Vetaal to a certain ascetic.

But Vetaal was clever.

Whenever Vikramaditya carried him through the forest, the spirit would tell him a strange story.

And at the end of every story, Vetaal would ask a difficult question.

There was, however, a terrible condition.

If King Vikramaditya knew the answer but refused to speak, his head would burst into a thousand pieces.

But if he answered the question correctly, Vetaal would immediately fly back to the tree.

Thus, the king was forced to begin his journey again and again.

And so, one night, as Vikramaditya carried Vetaal upon his shoulder, the spirit began another tale.

---

The Princess Mandaravati

“Listen carefully, O King,” said Vetaal.

“Long ago, there lived a beautiful princess named Mandaravati.

She was the daughter of a noble king, and her beauty was famed throughout the land.

Many young men wished to marry her.

Among them were three young Brahmins, each of whom fell deeply in love with her.

The three were not merely attracted to her beauty.

Each loved her with such devotion that they believed no other man could love her as truly as they did.

But destiny had written another story for them.

One day, Mandaravati fell gravely ill.

Her illness worsened with every passing day.

Physicians were summoned.

Medicines were prepared.

Prayers were offered.

But nothing could save her.

At last, the princess died.

The news spread through the kingdom like a dark cloud.

The palace that had once been filled with music and celebration was suddenly filled with sorrow.

The three young men were devastated.

The woman whom each had hoped to marry was now gone forever.

They could not accept their fate.

Yet each expressed his grief in a different manner.”

---

The First Man

“The first young man was so overcome with sorrow that he could not bear to leave Mandaravati.

He remained beside her body until the final rites were performed.

When her body was cremated, he stayed near the funeral pyre.

When the flames died down and nothing remained but ashes, he refused to leave.

He gathered the ashes and kept them carefully.

Then he built himself a small hut beside the cremation ground.

And there he remained.

Day after day.

Night after night.

He slept upon the ashes of Mandaravati.

He ate little.

He spoke to no one.

His only thought was of the woman he had loved.

He had nothing left of her but ashes, yet to him those ashes were more precious than gold.

He guarded them as a husband might guard the memory of his beloved.”

---

The Second Man

“The second young man could not bear to see Mandaravati's remains scattered and forgotten.

He collected her bones from the funeral pyre.

He carried them with him and journeyed to the sacred river Ganga.

He believed that by immersing her bones in the holy river, he would perform a final act of devotion and ensure that her soul received the proper rites.

And so he travelled far from his home.

Through forests and villages he went, carrying the bones of Mandaravati.

His journey was long and difficult.

Yet he did not complain.

For he believed that this was his final duty toward the woman he had loved.”

---

The Third Man

“The third young man was consumed by grief in an entirely different way.

He could not accept that Mandaravati was truly gone.

He wandered from place to place, searching for some miracle that might restore her life.

During his travels, he came upon the house of a learned Brahmin.

This Brahmin possessed a secret.

In his possession was a sacred book containing a powerful mantra known as the Mrita Sanjeevini — a sacred formula said to possess the power to restore life to the dead.

The young man learned of this secret book.

And one night, unable to overcome his longing for Mandaravati, he took the book and fled.

He hurried back toward the place where Mandaravati had been cremated.

He did not know whether the spell would truly work.

He did not know whether the gods would permit the dead to return to the world of the living.

But hope had taken hold of his heart.

And sometimes, hope can make a man attempt the impossible.”

---

The Three Meet Again

“By a strange turn of fate, the three young men came together once more.

The first was still guarding Mandaravati's ashes.

The second had returned after taking her bones to the sacred Ganga.

And the third arrived carrying the sacred book.

There they stood together, in the very place where their beloved Mandaravati had been cremated.

The third man opened the book.

He instructed the others to gather the remains.

The ashes were placed carefully.

The bones were brought forth.

Then the third man began to recite the sacred mantra.

The night grew still.

The flames of the funeral ground flickered in the darkness.

The three men watched in silence.

And then—

something extraordinary happened.

A strange white vapour began to rise from the ashes.

It gathered slowly.

At first it was nothing more than mist.

Then it began to take shape.

The three men stared in disbelief.

The form became clearer.

The bones and ashes seemed to disappear into it.

And before their astonished eyes—

Mandaravati returned to life.

She opened her eyes.

She breathed.

She stood before them once more, alive and beautiful as she had been before her death.

The three men were overwhelmed with joy.

But their joy did not last long.”

---

The Dispute

“Soon, a quarrel began.

Each man believed that he had the greatest claim to Mandaravati.

The first man said:

«‘I remained beside her ashes when everyone else had gone.
I slept upon the very ground where her body had been reduced to ashes.
My devotion never wavered.
Therefore, she should be my wife.’»

The second man replied:

«‘But I carried her bones to the sacred Ganga.
I protected what remained of her body and performed the final duty that love demanded of me.
Without her bones, how could she have been restored?’»

Then the third man spoke:

«‘Neither of you could have brought Mandaravati back.
I obtained the sacred knowledge and recited the mantra that restored her life.
Without me, she would still be dead.
Therefore, I have the greatest claim to her.’»

And thus the three men began arguing.

Each believed that his sacrifice was greater.

Each believed that he had earned the right to marry Mandaravati.

But who was right?

Who truly deserved her hand?”

---

Vetaal's Question

Vetaal turned toward King Vikramaditya.

His voice became serious.

«“Now, O King, I ask thee this:

Of these three men, who truly deserves to marry Mandaravati?

Is it the man who guarded her ashes?

Is it the man who carried her bones to the sacred Ganga?

Or is it the man who learned the spell and brought her back to life?”»

Vikramaditya remained silent for a moment.

He thought carefully.

He knew that if he answered, Vetaal would escape.

But he also knew the terrible consequence of remaining silent when he knew the truth.

At last, the king spoke.

---

Vikramaditya's Judgment

«“The answer is clear.”»

“The man who brought Mandaravati back to life cannot be her husband.

Why?

Because he gave her life anew.

He is, in a sense, like her father — the one who gave her a second birth.

A man cannot marry one whom he has brought into the world as a daughter.

Nor can the second man be her husband.

He carried her bones to the sacred Ganga and performed the final duties owed to the dead.

In the customs of that age, such duties were associated with a son.

Therefore, he too stands in the position of a son, not a husband.

But the first man—

he is different.

He did not give her new life.

He did not perform the duties of a son.

He simply remained faithful to her.

Even when there was no hope that she would ever return.

He slept beside her ashes.

He guarded the last remains of the woman he loved.

His devotion was that of a husband toward his beloved.

Therefore, of the three men, the first man is the one who deserves to marry Mandaravati.”

---

Vetaal's Escape

For a moment, the forest was silent.

Then Vetaal burst into laughter.

«“Well answered, O King Vikramaditya!”»

But Vikramaditya knew what would happen next.

The moment the king had spoken his answer—

Vetaal slipped from his shoulder.

With a strange, ghostly laugh, the spirit flew through the darkness.

Up through the trees he went.

And once again, he returned to the very same tree from which Vikramaditya had taken him.

The king stood there for a moment.

He sighed.

He knew what he had to do.

He turned around.

He walked back toward the tree.

He climbed it once more.

He seized Vetaal.

And placed the spirit upon his shoulder.

The strange game would begin again.

---

The Moral

The story of Mandaravati is not simply a tale of resurrection and magic.

Vetaal's question is actually a question about love, duty, sacrifice, and relationships.

All three men loved Mandaravati.

But they expressed their love differently.

The first showed devotion.

The second showed duty.

The third showed knowledge and determination.

Yet Vikramaditya did not simply ask:

«“Who did the most?”»

He asked:

«“What was the nature of each man's relationship with Mandaravati?”»

And that distinction changed everything.

The man who revived her was like a father.

The man who performed the final rites was like a son.

Only the man who remained beside her ashes continued to occupy the place of a lover.

And therefore, he was the one who could become her husband.

---

Closing

And perhaps that is what makes the tale of Vikram and Vetaal so enduring.

It does not merely ask us to choose between right and wrong.

It asks us to think.

Three men may perform three different acts of love, yet those acts may carry three completely different meanings.

As Vetaal's tales remind us:

«“A deed must not be judged by its greatness alone, but by the heart, the duty, and the relationship behind it.”»

And so the king walked once more into the darkness, with Vetaal upon his shoulder—

while the ancient forest whispered around them,

and somewhere beyond the trees,

another question awaited him.
